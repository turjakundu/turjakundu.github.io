Research Vision

Latency decides whether a prediction is usable. Interpretability decides whether it is trusted. In scientific computing, neither is optional. Yet modern graph learning relies on repeated neighborhood propagation that is expensive to serve, hard to interpret, and difficult to maintain as data evolves. My research asks a single question: how can graph structure be transformed into an explicit, interpretable, and computationally efficient representation for learning?

Graphs are the native representation for much of computational science. Particle detectors reconstruct tracks and classify events from hit graphs; biologists predict function from protein-interaction and molecular graphs; numerical libraries order and precondition sparse matrices by treating them as graphs. Outside science the same abstraction carries knowledge bases and fraud detection. In nearly all of them the graph is large, it changes, and an answer is wanted quickly — exactly where propagation-based models are hardest to deploy.

Graph structure often carries information that node attributes do not, yet most systems access it through message passing, coupling every prediction to the adjacency matrix. The cost is paid twice: in latency, because each query must reassemble a neighborhood, and in interpretability, because structural evidence dissolves into hidden states. I instead transform topology into explicit, inspectable features, selected on evidence of what each one contributes.

Research Contributions

1
Explicit Structural Representations for Graph Learning
Primary contribution

ATLAS (Adaptive Topology-based Learning at Scale) is a propagation-free framework that extracts topology as explicit channels before learning begins: multi-resolution community assignments, one-hop neighborhood features, and label-derived structural priors, all fed with node attributes to a compact multilayer perceptron. Training uses independent minibatches, and inference requires no neighborhood expansion.

Which channels belong is the substantive problem, which I addressed with a finite-sample information-theoretic criterion: a channel earns inclusion exactly when the label information it contributes exceeds the estimation cost it introduces. The criterion predicts an interior optimum in community resolution and reframes when structure helps at all — what matters is whether classes induce distinguishable connection patterns, not whether neighbors share labels, so edge homophily poorly predicts learnability.

Across 19 benchmarks, ATLAS attains the lowest average rank among 17 competing methods at medium scale and matches the best scalable baseline at million-node scale. On ogbn-products, inference completes in roughly half a second against 83 and 66 seconds for the strongest sampling-based competitors. Because each channel is a named quantity, the structural basis of a prediction stays recoverable.

Papers
  • Under Review ATLAS: Adaptive Topology-based Learning at Scale for Homophilic and Heterophilic Graphs — Turja Kundu, Sanjukta Bhowmick
  • SIAM 2025 Effect of Graph Structure vs. Feature Quality for GCN — Turja Kundu, Sanjukta Bhowmick (poster)
2
Low-Latency Inference for Scientific Computing
Active

HIOS is a hierarchical inter-operator scheduler for multi-GPU inference. It exploits a measured crossover: large operators contend for GPU resources under naive parallelization while small ones benefit from it. HIOS therefore spreads large operators across devices, groups small ones within each device, and maps the longest remaining path onto a single GPU. Built on CUDA-aware MPI and cuDNN, it cuts latency by up to 16.5% over the prior state of the art.

At Fermilab, under Dr. Giuseppe Cerati, I built an inference-as-a-service pipeline integrating LArSoft with NuSONIC’s Triton platform for the Convolutional Visual Network used in ICARUS, SBN, and DUNE event classification, characterizing latency and throughput against in-process execution.

Papers
  • IEEE Cluster 2023 HIOS: Hierarchical Inter-Operator Scheduler for Real-Time Inference of DAG-Structured Deep Learning Models on Multiple GPUs — Turja Kundu, Tao Shu
  • SC Workshops 2023 Accuracy-Constrained Efficiency Optimization and GPU Profiling of CNN Inference — Y. Zhang, D. Pandey, D. Wu, Turja Kundu, R. Li, T. Shu
3
Graph Structure Under Perturbation
Collaborative

In collaborative work at ICCS 2025, we generalized the rich club to scattered rich clubs and introduced a degree-of-scatteredness metric. Across ten real-world networks the metric spans nearly its full range, showing that networks differ fundamentally in resilience under targeted attack, and that sensitivity is often localized to regions we recover by snowball sampling in a single pass rather than by exact centrality.

Papers
  • ICCS 2025 Centrality Resilience in Complex Networks — F. A. Irany, Turja Kundu, S. Sarakar, A. Mukherjee, Sanjukta Bhowmick (short paper)

Research Agenda

I
Selecting and Maintaining Structural Representations
Active

ATLAS answered the statistical half of this question for one family of summaries. Generalizing across families, and keeping the answer valid as graphs change, is the work ahead. Core-periphery organization, biconnected components, k-core structure, and spectral coordinates each impose a different label prior, so I will generalize the ATLAS inclusion criterion into a selection rule spanning summary families.

Efficiency is the binding obstacle: pipelines compute summaries independently despite shared work, and ATLAS pays one community-detection run per resolution — the dominant expense at million-node scale. Because real partitions are not strictly nested, I will characterize when partial hierarchy can recover a full resolution profile in far fewer passes.

Selection alone is not enough. Scientific and operational graphs are rarely static, with detectors streaming new events and knowledge and transaction graphs growing daily, yet current systems recompute from scratch whenever structure changes. I will develop update algorithms that localize recomputation to the region a graph edit affects, with criteria for when a representation is still accurate enough to reuse. ATLAS supplies a starting point: I proved a stability bound on how far the label information carried by a community family can shift when the family changes, and extending it to genuine graph evolution is my next result.

The target is a diagnostic that answers, before training begins, whether a graph contains structural signal, which representations carry it, what they cost, and how long they stay valid.

II
Parallel Graph Algorithms and Performance Modeling on Accelerators
Active

Both halves of Thrust I depend on algorithms fast enough for the scale at which data arrives. I am developing a GPU algorithm for biconnected component decomposition that replaces the serialization of classical depth-first traversal with multiple concurrent traversals, and I intend to redesign connectivity, decomposition, and traversal primitives more broadly for accelerators.

Beyond individual kernels, I will build performance models for graph workloads. HIOS shows that the right parallelization strategy inverts once operator size passes a hardware-dependent threshold, and the analogous thresholds for structural preprocessing are unknown. Since a graph and its summary can differ in size by orders of magnitude, these models will guide placement and scheduling.

III
Is the Knowledge Inside a Language Model Graph-Structured?
Planned

Language models are used as knowledge bases, yet what they know is stored implicitly, so no one can say in advance which questions a model will answer reliably. Recent work makes a first measurement: prompt a model on individual facts from a knowledge graph, score each entity by how many of its facts the model gets right, and neighboring entities score alike — more so than randomly matched entities of equal degree. The organization is real, but it is measured on a graph we supplied.

My question is whether a model has a knowledge structure of its own. I will recover a graph from the model’s own answers rather than from Wikidata, and test how far the two agree. Related questions follow: do the regions a model does not know form communities at a particular scale, or scatter? Which relation types carry the correlation? Does a fact injected by fine-tuning spread to graph neighbors? And how few probes suffice to map a model of a given size?

The result is an audit that reports where a model is weak before deployment, and which queries to send to retrieval instead.

Entry Points for Student Researchers

This agenda is designed to be accessible to undergraduates. Propagation-free frameworks train on a single GPU, and many structural analyses run independently of model training. Each thrust admits semester-scale entry points:

  • Thrust I — measure how much a single structural summary contributes across a handful of graphs.
  • Thrust II — GPU kernel-level work with a visible deliverable, such as a parallel k-core implementation benchmarked against a serial baseline.
  • Thrust III — measure knowledge homophily in Knowledge graph.

I mentored two undergraduates through the CRA UR2PhD program in summer 2025 and completed its mentor training. My aim is a workshop paper or poster for each student. More on how I work with students is on the teaching page.
