Teaching Philosophy

I believe learning happens when students do the thinking, not when they watch me think. My job as an instructor is to design the conditions for that: a motivating question, a clear path, and enough support so that every student in the room can take the next step. In practice this means scaffolding — setting a task just beyond what a student can already do alone, supporting the gap, and removing that support once it is no longer needed.

This belief comes from my own path. I studied computer science in a second language, worked seven years as a software engineer, then returned to graduate school. Each stage taught me that understanding is built slowly, through practice and feedback, and that a good teacher makes that process visible.

My research is in high-performance computing and scalable graph learning, at the point where algorithms meet the machines that run them. That shapes what I teach. A student who can prove a bound but cannot explain why the asymptotically faster algorithm loses on real hardware has only half the picture, so I teach the analysis and the machine behavior together rather than as two separate subjects.

Lab sessions. Thirty minutes is a strict budget. It forced me to decide what students needed before they could start writing code, and to leave everything else out. I built each lecture around one idea and showed it with a live example I typed in front of the class — mistakes included, since watching me read an error message and form a guess shows beginners that confusion is part of programming.

Other duties. Office hours, assignment design, test cases and rubrics, grading, and exam reviews across all ten courses. Much of that time went to one-on-one debugging, where I ask students to explain what they expect each line to do before we run anything.

Teaching and Mentoring Methods

  • Start with a question worth answering. I open a topic with a concrete problem, not a definition, so the method arrives as the answer to something students already care about.
  • Scaffold, then fade. Tasks just past what a student can do alone, supported by templates and worked examples. The goal of every scaffold is its own removal.
  • Match the technique to the knowledge. Worked examples for procedures. Contrasting cases for concepts — two examples differing in exactly one respect, such as the same loop nest with its indices swapped, one fitting in cache and one not.
  • Concrete before abstract, structure in view. A small runnable example, then a picture, then the notation. I give the framework before the details, and reconnect the pieces every few weeks.
  • Assess understanding through artifacts. Nodding is not data. I ask for code, a diagram, a paragraph, or a spoken explanation.
  • Feedback that is specific, timely, understandable, actionable, and non-threatening. Many small assignments rather than a few large ones, written the way a good code reviewer writes: what works, what does not, what to try next — addressed to the work, never the person.
  • Make asking normal and setbacks ordinary. I learned computer science in a second language, so I remember being the student who does not want to ask. I use plain language, define jargon on first use, and share my own dead ends.

Mentoring Undergraduate Researchers

In Summer 2025 I mentored two undergraduates through the CRA UR2PhD program, after completing its mentor training. Neither had read a research paper closely or run an experiment of their own.

Expectations first. Many mentoring relationships fail before any research happens, in a quiet mismatch between what the mentor pictured and what the mentee pictured. We began by writing down what the project was, what a week of it would look like, how often we would meet, and what I expected them to produce, and revised that document through the summer.

Scaffolding, then removing it. We started with one paper and guiding questions, then reproducing a single figure from an existing codebase, then their own experiments. I asked for artifacts rather than status reports — a diagram, a plot, a paragraph in their own words — because an artifact is the only honest way to assess understanding, and it gives a student something tangible early. By the end of the summer they were presenting their own results and defending their own design choices.

Setbacks and diagnosis. A new undergraduate reads bugs and failed experiments as a verdict on whether they belong, so I tell mentees about my own failures. When progress stalls, the easy explanation is about character, and it is usually wrong and never actionable. I name what is happening, watch for a pattern, and generate several causes before choosing one: the step may be too large, the student may have no group to belong to, or something outside the project may have their attention. I also know when a problem is past what a near-peer mentor should handle alone.

Undergraduate research is teaching, and the main skill is knowing how much support to remove, and when. I would welcome the chance to build a small undergraduate research group around scalable graph algorithms and machine learning systems. Concrete starting projects are on the research page.

What Industry Experience Brings

Before my PhD I spent seven years as a software engineer in Dhaka, Bangladesh. I built SIP relay and proxy servers with pthreads and BSD sockets, developed distributed service-oriented systems, and served as Assistant Director (IT) at ASA International, where our web-based microfinance banking systems served millions of borrowers across a large branch network. Three things from that work go directly into a classroom.

  • Systems examples from production code. Threads, sockets, file descriptors, and signals are the syllabus of a systems or Linux programming course, and I spent years writing them for a living. A concurrency bug I actually had to find teaches more than a constructed one, and I can tell students what the fix cost.
  • Requirements and design. The hardest part of those banking systems was learning what branch staff meant by words they used differently than we did, and I have watched one week’s schema decision constrain a system for five years. Both make good assignments.
  • Running team projects and capstones. I know how to break a system into pieces a team can divide, and how to set milestones that surface problems in week four rather than week fourteen. Version control discipline, code review, and writing code a teammate can read six months later are habits learned by practicing them under supervision, not by being told about them.
